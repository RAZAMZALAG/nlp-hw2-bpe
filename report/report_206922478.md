## Method
All three tokenizers use a word-level Byte Pair Encoding tokenizer (Sennrich/HF style, as in the
lecture). Each line is split on whitespace and every non-initial word is prefixed with a space
marker `▁`; the vocabulary is initialized with all characters and then the most frequent adjacent
symbol pair is merged repeatedly (computed with incremental pair-counts over a unique-word frequency
table, so full domain_1 trains in ~12 min) until the vocabulary reaches 2000. **Encoding uses greedy
longest-match (maximal-munch) over the trained vocabulary via a prefix trie** — O(len) per word with a
per-word cache; decoding joins token surfaces and maps `▁` back to a space.

Differences from textbook BPE: (1) a bounded **bigram phase** adds the few most frequent adjacent
word-pairs as single tokens (e.g. `▁in▁the`), which satisfies the mandatory "≥ 1 bigram" rule while
**capping tokens at the bigram level** (at most one internal space / two words); (2) **length-preserving
`[UNK]` decoding** — an unseen character decodes to a single replacement character, so token↔word
character-span alignment in the NER pipeline never shifts; (3) case is preserved, since capitalization
is the dominant entity cue on these data; (4) encoding is **decoupled from merge-replay** — greedy
longest-match over the final vocab gives the same legal segmentation far faster (see Competition).

## Tokenizers and the hidden domain
T1 is trained on domain_1 (Twitter), T2 on domain_2 (news). T3 targets a hidden domain and is trained
on domain_1 + domain_2 combined for maximum character and subword coverage. The vocabulary is kept
small (2000) deliberately: a data analysis showed ≈ 68 % of dev entities are out-of-vocabulary, and
small **shared** subwords generalize to unseen words far better than rare whole-word tokens — a whole
token for a never-seen entity is an embedding the fixed NER model never trained. Length-preserving
`[UNK]` further protects T3 against unknown characters in the hidden domain.

## Most / least common bigrams (training set)
- **domain_1** — most: `▁in▁the`, `▁for▁the`, `▁going▁to`, `▁to▁be`, `▁on▁the`;
  least (freq 1): `▁hockey▁3-0.`, `▁ta▁so`, `▁Queen▁will`, `▁voice,▁as`, `@officialSusanB▁ta`.
- **domain_2** — most: `▁of▁the`, `▁in▁the`, `▁to▁the`, `▁on▁the`, `▁to▁be`;
  least (freq 1): `▁a▁Wonder`, `▁10-year-old▁self`, `▁Kids:▁Reflections`, `▁Reflections▁During`, `Giving▁Culture`.

## Validation (dev sets) and parameter selection
At the chosen vocab 2000: **T1 (domain_1) F1 0.469**, tok/char 0.339; **T2 (domain_2) F1 0.961**,
tok/char 0.358. Both clear the 0.4 threshold; domain_2 has a large margin.

Vocabulary was chosen by a full sweep (num_bigrams = 5, full-data training, 20-epoch NER):

| vocab | domain_1 F1 | domain_2 F1 | domain_1 tok/char | domain_2 tok/char |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1500 | 0.473 | 0.960 | 0.361 | 0.390 |
| **2000** | **0.469** | **0.961** | 0.339 | 0.358 |
| 3000 | 0.450 | 0.961 | 0.314 | 0.321 |

This is an efficiency↔F1 trade: larger vocab compresses better but lowers domain_1 F1 (smaller
vocabularies force rare/entity words into *shared* subwords that generalize to the 68 %-OOV dev set).
We pick **2000** as the balanced operating point — best generalization proxy at competitive
compression; 1500 is dominated, 3000 trades 4 % F1 for ~8 % compression. A `num_bigrams` sweep
(5→200) gave essentially no compression gain and slightly more entity fragmentation, so we keep 5.

## Competition: encoding speed
Switching encoding from merge-replay (O(W²) per word) to **greedy longest-match over the final vocab**
(prefix trie, O(W)) speeds up cold encoding by **+54 % on domain_1 (363k→560k tok/s) and +64 % on
domain_2 (430k→706k tok/s)** with slightly fewer tokens. The segmentation differs, costing a small F1
amount (T1 0.469→0.460, T2 0.961→0.960, both well above the 0.4 gate); given the equal-weight
speed/efficiency/F1 competition, the large speed gain outweighs the ~2 % F1 dip, so longest-match is the
shipped encoder.

## Hidden-domain (T3) evaluation
T3 trains on **all** provided data (domain_1 + domain_2). We tested a balanced alternative
(downsampling domain_1 to domain_2's size to de-bias the vocabulary); it **lost** on both dev proxies
(domain_1 F1 0.488→0.469, domain_2 0.964→0.961) — discarding 75 % of domain_1 costs more in coverage
than the de-biasing gains, so the full-data mix is kept. Notably T3's domain_1 F1 (0.488) exceeds T1's
(0.469): more data yields better shared-subword coverage. For the unknown hidden domain we therefore
expect solid F1 — maximal subword coverage plus length-preserving `[UNK]` give every word a trainable,
alignment-safe first subtoken regardless of domain. The main limiter is the ambiguous capitalization
cue (capitalized tokens begin both entities and non-entities), a property of the fixed model and the
small, noisy data rather than of the tokenizer.
