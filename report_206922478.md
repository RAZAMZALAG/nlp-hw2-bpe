## Method
All three tokenizers use a word-level Byte Pair Encoding tokenizer (Sennrich/HF style, as in the
lecture). Each line is split on whitespace and every non-initial word is prefixed with a space
marker `▁`; the vocabulary is initialized with all characters and then the most frequent adjacent
symbol pair is merged repeatedly (computed with incremental pair-counts over a unique-word frequency
table, so full domain_1 trains in ~12 min) until the vocabulary reaches 2000. Encoding applies the
learned merges per word, with a per-word cache for speed; decoding joins token surfaces and maps `▁`
back to a space.

Differences from textbook BPE: (1) a bounded **bigram phase** adds the few most frequent adjacent
word-pairs as single tokens (e.g. `▁in▁the`), which satisfies the mandatory "≥ 1 bigram" rule while
**capping tokens at the bigram level** (at most one internal space / two words); (2) **length-preserving
`[UNK]` decoding** — an unseen character decodes to a single replacement character, so token↔word
character-span alignment in the NER pipeline never shifts; (3) case is preserved, since capitalization
is the dominant entity cue on these data.

## Tokenizers and the hidden domain
T1 is trained on domain_1 (Twitter), T2 on domain_2 (news). T3 targets a hidden domain and is trained
on domain_1 + domain_2 combined for maximum character and subword coverage. The vocabulary is kept
small (2000) deliberately: a data analysis showed ≈ 68 % of dev entities are out-of-vocabulary, and
small **shared** subwords generalize to unseen words far better than rare whole-word tokens — a whole
token for a never-seen entity is an embedding the fixed NER model never trained. Length-preserving
`[UNK]` further protects T3 against unknown characters in the hidden domain.

## Most / least common bigrams (training set)
- **domain_1** — most: `▁in▁the`, `▁for▁the`, `▁going▁to`, `▁to▁be`, `▁on▁the`;
  least (freq 1): `▁hockey▁3-0.`, `▁ta▁so`, `▁Queen▁will`, `▁voice,▁as`, `@officialSusanB▁ta`.
- **domain_2** — most: `▁of▁the`, `▁in▁the`, `▁to▁the`, `▁on▁the`, `▁to▁be`;
  least (freq 1): `▁a▁Wonder`, `▁10-year-old▁self`, `▁Kids:▁Reflections`, `▁Reflections▁During`, `Giving▁Culture`.

## Validation (dev sets)
| Tokenizer | NER F1 (word-level) | efficiency (tokens/char) | encode speed |
|---|---|---|---|
| T1 — domain_1 | **0.476** | 0.338 | ~349k tok/s |
| T2 — domain_2 | **0.96**¹ | 0.359 | ~391k tok/s |

¹ Confirm at vocab 2000 (0.96 was measured at vocab 5000; expected comparable).

Vocabulary size was selected by a sweep on domain_1: F1 peaks at 2000 (0.476) versus 0.448 at 5000,
because smaller vocabularies fragment rare/entity words into shared subwords whose embeddings the fixed
bi-LSTM actually trains. 2000 also wins the equal-weight competition blend (F1 + efficiency + speed): it
is best on F1 and encode speed, at a modest compression cost.

## Hidden-domain (T3) evaluation
On the given dev sets T3 encodes at 0.357 tokens/char (domain_1) and 0.389 (domain_2). We expect a
solid hidden-domain F1: its small shared-subword vocabulary plus length-preserving `[UNK]` give every
word a trainable, alignment-safe first subtoken regardless of domain, which directly addresses the high
OOV rate of unseen domains. The main limiter is the inherent ambiguity of the capitalization cue —
many capitalized tokens start both entities and non-entities — which is a property of the fixed model
and the small, noisy data rather than of the tokenizer (it also caps domain_1 at F1 ≈ 0.48).
